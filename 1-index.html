Start here

This site teaches the CFOP speedcubing method the way you wanted to learn it: every idea built from first principles, every term in the glossary, visuals wherever they help, and as little raw memorization as possible. The goal is not to recall 119 algorithms. The goal is to understand what the moves do well enough that you could work most of them out yourself.

The bargain: you can solve the cube with 0 memorized algorithms of understanding plus a handful of short "triggers" your fingers learn on their own. Full CFOP has 41 F2L cases, 57 OLL cases and 21 PLL cases. This site shows how those numbers collapse into a few ideas: three insertion patterns for F2L, two orientation families for OLL, and one permutation machine (the commutator) for PLL.

How to use it: read Foundations once, slowly, with a cube in your hand. Then work through the four CFOP stages in order. Whenever a word is unfamiliar, jump to the Glossary and come back. The Learning path tells you what to drill and when you are ready to move on.

Foundations

Piece anatomy: the cube is 20 pieces, not 54 stickers

A solved-looking cube is made of three kinds of pieces, and each kind can only ever live in its own kind of position:

  • 6 centers - one sticker each. They are fixed to the core and only spin in place.
  • 12 edges - two stickers each. An edge can only ever be an edge.
  • 8 corners - three stickers each. A corner can only ever be a corner.

Centers (highlighted)

Edges (highlighted) - corners are the rest

First principle 1: you never move a sticker, you move a piece. When a face turns, 8 stickers change position but it is really 4 corners and 4 edges taking a ride together. Solving means sending each of the 20 movable pieces to its one correct home, facing the right way.

The center rule: the color scheme never changes

Because centers are fixed to the core, the white center is always opposite the yellow center, green is always opposite blue, and red is always opposite orange. On a standard cube held with white on top and green facing you, red is on the right. So every piece has exactly one home: the white-green edge belongs between the white and green centers, forever. You never decide where a piece goes. The centers already decided.

Move notation: the whole language

Every algorithm is written in six letters. A letter means: turn that face one quarter turn clockwise, as if you were looking straight at that face. A letter with a prime (') means counterclockwise. A letter with 2 means a half turn (direction does not matter).

R - right face

Clockwise looking at the right face. The front-right column travels up to the top.

R' - right, inverted

Counterclockwise. The front-right column travels down to the bottom.

U - up face

Clockwise looking down at the top. The front top row slides to the left side.

F - front face

Clockwise looking at the front. The bottom row of the top face moves to the right face.

L - left face

Clockwise looking at the left face (net shows the result of one L). The lit strip is what moved.

D / B - down and back

Same rule, seen from below and from behind. Result of one D shown. You rarely need these in CFOP.

  • The standard color scheme is used everywhere: white opposite yellow, green opposite blue, red opposite orange.
  • Stage diagrams (Cross through PLL) are drawn in the standard solving orientation: white on the bottom, yellow on top, so the last layer you are working on always faces up.
  • Faded stickers are pieces that do not matter for the idea being shown. Bright stickers are the ones to watch.
  • An arrow on a face means: turn that face in the arrow's direction.
  • Algorithm cards show the case first, then the moves. Every diagram on this site was generated by a real cube simulator, so what you see is exactly what the moves produce.

What a move really is (the idea behind every algorithm)

One face turn moves 4 corners in a cycle and 4 edges in a cycle. Do the same turn four times and every piece is home again: the turn has order 4. This single fact explains all of speedcubing math:

Try it: hold a solved cube and repeat R U R' U' slowly. After exactly 6 repetitions the cube is solved again. Every sequence of moves has a finite order - repeat it enough and you always come home. Algorithms are not magic strings; they are loops with a known length.

An algorithm "works" when its loop moves almost everything home but leaves a small, useful change: three corners swapped, two corners twisted, and so on. The art of CFOP is finding loops whose leftover change is exactly the problem in front of you. In the PLL section you will build one of these loops from scratch.

One more foundation for later: you cannot twist a single corner. Corner twists always balance out - the total twist of all corners is always a multiple of one full turn. If one corner is twisted, another one is twisted too. This is why OLL corner cases come in families, and why a flipped corner on a "solved" cube means someone took it apart.

The CFOP method

CFOP solves the cube in four stages: Cross, F2L (first two layers), OLL (orient the last layer), PLL (permute the last layer). It is the method used by nearly every top speedcuber.

Why this order? Because each stage shrinks the problem

  • Cross: solve the 4 bottom edges. 8 pieces of the bottom layer - you place 4 of them. Intuition only, no algorithms.
  • F2L: solve the 4 bottom corners together with the 4 middle edges, as 4 corner-edge pairs. The whole bottom two layers, done with a few repeating ideas.
  • OLL: the top face is a mess of colors. Make it one solid color (orient every top piece), without caring yet which piece is where.
  • PLL: the top face is solid but the pieces are in the wrong spots. Move them home (permute) without disturbing the solved two layers or the solid top.
First principle 2: orientation before permutation. It is far easier to make every top sticker face up first (a color problem) and only then shuffle pieces home (a position problem), because a solid top face lets you read positions at a glance. That split is what makes the last layer learnable at all.

Stage 1: Cross

Goal: the four white edges on the bottom, each one also matched to its side center.

What "solved" means here: look at the bottom face - all four white edge stickers are down. Now look at the side faces: the bottom row of every side matches its center. An edge has two stickers, and both must be right. A white edge with the wrong side color is not placed, it is parked.

Diagrams from here on use the solving orientation: white bottom, yellow top.

The principle: an edge has two stickers, so solve it in one move, not two

Beginners put a white edge on the bottom face and then fix the side color as a second step. Instead, line the edge's side sticker up with its center first, then drop it home with a single turn of that side face. Every cross edge can be placed in one move once it is aligned - the whole skill of the cross is getting all four aligned at the same time without knocking out the ones you already placed.

A worked example

Scramble: R' F D' - three white edges are disturbed. Watch each repair move place one edge while leaving the placed ones alone:

How to practice (this is where speed is really born)

  • Plan before you touch. Competitions give 15 seconds of inspection time before the clock starts, and top solvers plan the entire cross in that window. Copy the habit: scramble, put the cube down, plan all four edges mentally, then solve the cross without looking away or pausing.
  • Solve the cross on the bottom from day one. It feels slower for a week. Then you can see the first F2L pair while finishing the cross, which is the single biggest speed gain in all of CFOP.
  • Measure yourself in moves, not seconds. A good cross takes 8 moves or fewer. Count yours. When you regularly plan a 6-to-8 move cross in inspection, you are ready to move on.

Stage 2: F2L - first two layers, as pairs

Goal: the four white corners and the four middle-layer edges, inserted as four corner-edge pairs into their four slots.

The concepts (everything else is these words combined)

  • Slot: the home of one corner plus its matching edge, between two side faces at the bottom. Four slots: front-right, front-left, back-right, back-left.
  • Pair: a corner and its matching edge, connected so they drop into the slot together. Building the pair is 90% of F2L.
  • Keyhole: an unsolved slot is a feature, not a problem - you can pass pieces through the empty space without disturbing anything solved.

The one trigger your fingers must learn

R U R' U' - the "sexy move".

Why it works: R lifts the front-right slot up into the top layer. U slides the top layer over. R' puts the slot back down - but now whatever sat on top has been swapped into it. U' restores the top. Four moves, and the slot has traded contents with the top layer while the rest of the cube is untouched. Every F2L insertion is this idea or its mirror, L' U' L U (the lefty version).

Repeat it 6 times and the cube returns to solved - feel that loop once and you understand it forever.

The three situations (all 41 cases are setups to these)

1. The pair is already built. Corner and edge are connected on top. Slide the pair over its slot with a U turn, then insert: U R U' R'

Derivation: nothing to fix, so just do the slot trade from the right angle.

2. Corner and edge are separate on top. Use one sexy move to kick them into each other so they connect into a pair, look, then insert as in case 1: R U R' U' then re-pair and insert.

Derivation habit: ask "how do I hide the corner (R), bring the edge next to it (U), and unhide (R')?"

3. Something is stuck in the slot wrong. An empty slot is a tool (keyhole), but a wrongly filled slot is a trap. Eject it with one sexy move - that pops the stuck pieces onto the top - then solve the freed pair: R U R' U' U R U' R'

First principle 3: you do not memorize F2L cases, you interrogate them. For every scramble of corner and edge, ask three questions: Is the pair built? If not, what one move connects them (usually hiding the corner with R or R' first)? Where is the slot? The answer is always a short sequence of hide, connect, insert. The 41 "cases" are just the 41 answers to those questions, and after a few weeks your hands answer on their own.

Finger tricks that matter here

  • Push R with your right wrist; push U and U' with your right index and right ring finger without re-gripping.
  • Never rotate the whole cube to avoid a lefty trigger - learn the mirror L' U' L U early.
  • Solve far pairs first and near pairs last - whatever remains unsolved is where you can still see.

Stage 3: OLL - make the top face one color

Goal: every yellow sticker facing up. Positions do not matter yet - only orientation.

The full step has 57 cases. You will not learn 57 cases. The 2-look version splits OLL into two smaller problems: first orient the edges (make a yellow cross on top), then orient the corners. Each half needs one idea family.

Look 1: orient the edges - three shapes

Ignore corners completely and look at the yellow pattern on top. You will always see one of three shapes (the yellow cross itself is the fourth - then you are done). Each has a fix built from the same idea: the front face turn pulls edge stickers up into place.

The line. A yellow bar across the middle. Hold it horizontal, then: F R U R' U' F'

Read it: F lifts the front edge slot, the sexy move R U R' U' fixes the middle edges, F' closes. It is the F2L trigger wearing a front-face jacket.

The L (hook). Two yellow edges making a corner. Hold it in the back-left as shown, then: F U R U' R' F' - the mirror of the line fix.

The dot. No yellow edges at all. Do the line fix, then the hook fix right after - the first creates the shape the second one solves: F R U R' U' F' f R U R' U' f'

Small f is the wide version: turn the front two layers together.

Look 2: orient the corners - the Sune family

Sune: R U R' U R U2 R'

Why it works: it is the sexy move with the last U' replaced by U2. That one change makes the loop leave the top layer in place but twist corners in a balanced way - remember, twists always cancel out in total, so Sune twists some corners one way and one corner back. Hold the cube so the twisted corner that needs twisting clockwise is front-right (the "headlight" yellow sticker faces you on the left of the front), and Sune finishes the yellow face.

Antisune is the mirror: R U2 R' U' R U' R'

The other corner cases: every remaining corner pattern is solved by Sune or Antisune, at most twice, from the right angle. The recognition trick: count the corners whose yellow faces up (0, 1, or 2 of them), and learn where the side-facing yellow stickers point. That is a page of patterns, not 57 algorithms. Graduate to full OLL later only if the second look starts costing you time you care about.

Stage 4: PLL - move the last pieces home

Goal: the top is solid yellow; now shuffle the top-layer pieces into place without breaking anything below.

The machine that builds every permutation: the commutator

Here is the deepest idea on this site, and the one that turns memorization into derivation. Suppose you have a little sequence A that does something useful to one small area - say, it swaps two corners inside the right layer and messes up other things. Now do this:

A, then B, then undo A, then undo B - written A B A' B'

A does its swap (plus collateral damage). B turns the top layer, carrying a different corner into the area A affects. A' undoes A - but the area now holds a different piece, so the undo completes a three-corner cycle instead of restoring. B' puts the top layer back. All the collateral damage canceled. The only survivors: a clean 3-cycle of corners.

Real example, derived not memorized: R U' L' U R' U' L U

This is A = R U' L' U (a four-move corner swap in the top), B = R'. Run it on a solved cube and check: exactly three corners have moved (front-right, back-left, back-right on top), every edge is untouched. Do it three times and the cube is solved again - order 3, exactly as a 3-cycle demands.

Change A and B and you manufacture any cycle you need. This is where PLL algorithms come from.

Conjugates complete the toolkit: if the pieces you want to cycle are not in the right places, do a setup move S that parks them there, run your commutator, then undo S. Setup - work - unsetup. Nearly all 21 PLL algorithms are a commutator or a conjugate of one.

How to recognize a PLL case

With the top solid, look at the side faces of the top layer. You are hunting for patterns:

  • Headlights: two corner stickers of the same color side by side on one face.
  • Blocks: a solved 2x1 chunk (corner plus edge matching) - a finished piece of the layer.
  • Bars: three matching stickers in a row.

Count headlights and blocks on all four sides and the case family is usually decided before you touch the cube.

2-look PLL: four algorithms cover everything

Step 1 - place the corners: the T-perm. R U R' U' R' F R2 U' R' U' R U R' F'

It swaps the two right-side top corners (plus two edges, which step 2 repairs). Rule: if any face shows headlights, put the headlights on your left and run it - corners land. If no face has headlights, run it once from anywhere to create headlights, then do it properly. It is the sexy move, an F turn, and a mirror - you already know its bones.

Step 2 - place the edges: the U-perms. Three edges cycle while one stays home: R U' R U R U R U' R' U' R2 or its mirror R2 U R U R' U' R' U' R' U R'

Find the one solved edge (a full bar on one side), put that side at the back, and run the version that cycles the other three the right way. Wrong direction? Run it again - a 3-cycle done twice is the reverse cycle.

Two more edge cases exist, and both are double swaps, so each is its own inverse:

H-perm: opposite edges swap

M2 U M2 U2 M2 U M2 - pure middle-slice work.

Z-perm: adjacent edges swap

M2 U M U2 M2 U2 M U' M2

Master track: read four famous PLLs as commutators

Once the machine clicks, the classic algorithms stop being strings and start being stories. Work these out on a solved cube and watch which pieces move:

Learning path: beginner to master

Beginner Weeks 1-3 - own the language

  • Notation fluency: read any algorithm and execute it without translating letter by letter.
  • Cross: intuitive, planned during inspection, on the bottom, under 8 moves.
  • F2L: the three situations, solved slowly with zero memorized cases. Deliberate "hide, connect, insert" out loud.
  • Finish with 2-look OLL and 2-look PLL. Full solves, no timer yet. Readiness check: 20 solves in a row with no stage ever stumping you.

Intermediate Months 1-3 - fluency

  • Lookahead: while inserting one pair, your eyes are already finding the next. Drill: solve F2L at a fixed slow tempo with no pauses - smoothness first, speed follows.
  • Cross plus first pair planned together in inspection.
  • Finger tricks: sexy move and its mirror without re-gripping; U pushes with both index fingers.
  • Readiness check: average of 12 solves under 45 seconds with 2-look.

Advanced Months 3-9 - efficiency

  • Full PLL (21 cases) in batches; instant recognition from two sides.
  • F2L from the back and left slots without cube rotations; keyhole inserts.
  • Start absorbing full OLL by shape family (squares, lightning bolts, the Sune family first).
  • Readiness check: sub-25 average, cross solved blind every time, no regrips in triggers.

Master Ongoing - derive, do not recall

  • Commutator fluency: given any three misplaced pieces, construct a working 3-cycle on the spot with the A B A' B' machine plus a setup move.
  • Algorithm forensics: take any algorithm from a cheat sheet, run it on a solved cube, and explain which pieces it moves and why the collateral cancels. If you can teach it, you own it.
  • Solve analysis: reconstruct your slow solves, find the wasted moves (most are in F2L), and set one efficiency goal per week.
  • Readiness check: you can invent a valid (if inelegant) solution to any PLL case without looking anything up.

Glossary

Algorithm
A written sequence of moves that produces a specific, small change (like a 3-cycle) while leaving everything else solved.
AUF (adjust U face)
A U, U' or U2 turn done before or after an algorithm to line the top layer up with the rest of the cube.
Center
The one-sticker piece at the middle of each face. Fixed to the core, so it defines the face's color permanently.
Commutator
The pattern A B A' B': do, setup, undo, un-setup. The collateral effects cancel and a clean small cycle survives. The engine behind most PLL algorithms.
Conjugate (setup moves)
The pattern S A S': park the target pieces where algorithm A can reach them, run A, then undo the parking move.
Corner
A three-sticker piece. The cube has 8. A corner can only ever occupy a corner position.
Cross
CFOP stage 1: the four bottom edges solved with their side colors matched.
Edge
A two-sticker piece. The cube has 12. An edge can only ever occupy an edge position.
F2L (first two layers)
CFOP stage 2: solving the four bottom corners and four middle edges as corner-edge pairs.
Finger tricks
Executing moves with finger pushes instead of whole-hand regrips, so triggers flow without pauses.
Headlights
Two same-colored corner stickers side by side on one side of the top layer. A key PLL recognition pattern.
Inspection
The 15 seconds competitions allow you to study the scrambled cube before the clock starts. Use it to plan the whole cross.
Keyhole
Using an empty (unsolved) slot as working space to pass pieces through without disturbing solved ones.
Last layer (LL)
The top layer when solving with the cross on the bottom. The subject of OLL and PLL.
Lookahead
Tracking the next pieces with your eyes while your hands finish the current step. The main source of speed after algorithms.
Move order
How many times you must repeat a move sequence to return to the starting state. A face turn has order 4; the sexy move has order 6.
OLL (orientation of the last layer)
CFOP stage 3: making the top face a single color. 57 cases in full; about 7 patterns in the 2-look version.
Orientation
Which way a piece is facing, independent of where it sits. A corner has 3 orientations, an edge has 2.
Pair
A corner connected to its matching edge, ready to be inserted into a slot as one unit.
Permutation
Which piece sits where, independent of which way it faces. PLL is a pure permutation step.
PLL (permutation of the last layer)
CFOP stage 4: moving the top-layer pieces home after the top face is solid. 21 cases in full; 4 algorithms in the 2-look version.
Prime (')
A suffix meaning counterclockwise: R' is R backwards. Also called inverse.
Scramble
A random sequence used to mix the cube, or the mixed state itself.
Sexy move
R U R' U' - the four-move trigger behind most F2L inserts and many OLL/PLL algorithms. Six repetitions return the cube to solved.
Sledgehammer
R' F R F' - the other common F2L/OLL trigger, the sexy move's front-face cousin.
Slice moves (M, E, S)
Turns of the middle layers only. M follows L's direction, E follows D's, S follows F's. Used in H and Z perms and some F2L tricks.
Slot
The paired home of one bottom corner and its middle edge. There are four; unsolved ones are working space (see keyhole).
Sune / Antisune
R U R' U R U2 R' and its mirror. Corner-orientation algorithms that cover the entire 2-look OLL corner step between them.
TPS (turns per second)
Raw turning speed. It buys you almost nothing without lookahead; fluency first, TPS follows.
Trigger
A short move sequence your fingers run as one gesture, like the sexy move. Algorithms are memorized as chains of triggers, not letters.
Wide move (Rw, Uw...)
A face turn that drags the neighboring inner layer with it. Sometimes written with lowercase letters (r, u).
x, y, z rotations
Re-gripping the whole cube in the direction of R, U, or F respectively. Not layer turns - they only change what you call front.
2-look OLL / 2-look PLL
Splitting a last-layer stage into two smaller sub-steps so a handful of algorithms covers every case.
